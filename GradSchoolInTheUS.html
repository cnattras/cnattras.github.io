General information on graduate school in physics in the US for foreigners

focusing on the graduate program in physics at UTK


Disclaimer: This information is a mixture of hard facts and my opinions and is provided for your information.  It should not be interpreted as endorsed by the University of Tennessee, Knoxville or the physics department.  While I will try to keep this information up to date and accurate, you should double check the accuracy, specifically the accuracy of dates and deadlines.  When I make comparisons to programs abroad, I'm generally comparing to an average of a European system.  Obviously this information cannot be complete and represents only one view point.

General overview of the US higher education system:
American high schools are not separated into levels the way they are in most of Europe, rather all students go to the same high schools - whether they are going to college or not.  (The words "College" and "University" are generally interchangeable in the US system.)  US high school students are not required to take physics or calculus.  It is not unusual for US college students to have never had physics before college and many physics majors have not had calculus before college.  Many US high schools, particularly in rural or less affluent areas, do not offer calculus.  US undergraduates therefore have not specialized at all before they reach the university.  Many of the classes US undergraduates take in their first year are roughly equivalent to classes that a European student who studied at a gymnasium or the equivalent would have had during high school.  The standard US university education is a liberal arts education, meaning that students are required to take classes well outside of their specialty.  More than 1/2 of the classes that a US physics major would take as an undergraduate may be classes other than math and science.  The average US physics bachelor's student has more experience in writing than the average European diploma student.  In most US physics classes, students are required to do homework weekly and the exam comprises a smaller fraction of the final grade than in most European systems.  US students are generally not required to do a thesis and if they do, they are generally at a lower level than the diploma theses in Europe.  Most American physics majors who are considering graduate studies in physics do summer research but are not likely to be engaged in research during the year.

The bachelor's degree is a four year degree, usually broken up into two semesters.  While the details vary depending on the program, the standard bachelor's program in physics at a US university consists of the following physics courses:
  • 1 year of a survey course which is a general overview of classical physics, comprising classical mechanics, geometrical optics, perhaps some thermodynamics and/or fluid dynamics, and electricity and magnetism.  Roughly comparable to what many gymnasiums would teach.
  • 1/2 year of modern physics, a survey course of quantum mechanics, relativity, and some other topics in modern physics (nuclear physics, solid state physics)
  • 1/2 year of thermodynamics
  • 1/2 year of optics (not in every program)
  • 1 year of quantum mechanics
  • 1 year of electricity and magnetism
  • 1 year of classical mechanics
  • 1 year of lab classes, usually separated into an electronics lab and a modern physics lab

Each of these 1/2 year classes would meet for 3-5 hours/week for about 15 weeks and have 2-3 exams.  Overall a student would take classes that meet 15-20 hours/week in any given semester.  You can read about the details of the physics major at UTK here.  The details may vary and physics majors who do not intend to go to graduate school may not have to take all of these classes.

Very few graduate programs in the US are for master's degrees only.  Instead the master's degree is usually attained en route to a PhD.  A standard PhD program consists of approximately two years of course work followed by an average of four years of dedicated research.  The courses required are usually:

  • 1/2 year of mathematical methods in physics
  • 1 year of quantum mechanics
  • 1 year of electricity and magnetism
  • 1 year of classical mechanics
  • Multiple courses on special topics, usually focusing on the student's research area

Some graduate programs are structured to have less course work and some more.  Generally programs labeled "Applied Physics" require fewer courses. 
In most classes, even graduate classes, you will be required to do homework weekly and take 2-3 exams.  You probably will not be able to pass your classes if you do not do the homework.  Details of the UTK graduate program in physics can be found here.

There is only rarely a hard limit on the number of years that a student can be a PhD student, but the average graduate student in the US spends about six years completing a PhD program.

The US bachelor's degree is not specialized.  This means that students may not know whether they want to study optics or nuclear physics when they apply to graduate school.

Most graduate students are supported by a teaching assistanceship in their first two years. This means that you are expected to teach undergraduate courses.  These are most commonly undergraduate laboratory courses for non-science majors.  After the first couple years students are usually supported on a research assistanceship, which means that you get paid to do research.  Full time graduate students also almost always get health insurance through the university and sometimes other benefits, such as access to the gym on campus or a bus card for the city.


Comparison to the "average" European system and what foreign students can expect
The average European university "diploma" would transfer to the US as a master's degree.  However, the actual level of the programs can vary and usually a European diploma is somewhere between a US bachelor's and a US master's degree.  Most foreign students will therefore score higher on the physics section of the graduate entrance exam, the GRE - but students coming in with the equivalent of a US master's degree are also expected to score better on this exam.  Since the average foreign student comes in with a slightly higher level degree, some foreign students can test out of some of the graduate courses.  Some universities require students to take all of the classes anyways.  Sometimes if you test out of classes, you only get out of taking those classes but you still have to take the same number of credits in order to graduate.  The details of the university's system are important when considering how your courses might transfer and how many years it might take you to earn your degree.

Most graduate programs have a qualifier exam.  The details of when this exam is administered depend on the university, but usually this is offered the August after a student is admitted.  Students usually get at least two chances to pass, but in most cases after failing the exam twice, students are not allowed to get a PhD and must either leave with a master's or leave without a degree.  In most cases the content of the qualifier exam is closely related to the content of the first year classes.  Therefore students should only try to get out of classes if they have truly mastered the material.  In most universities most students who do well in classes are able to pass the qualifier exam.  Students should beware of universities where the failure rate is high.

Since the US degree is not specialized, if you go to a graduate program in the US, even if you specialized for your diploma, you do not have to stick with the same specialty.  Most foreign students do, but it is not obligatory.

Almost all students, including foreign students, get financial support while in graduate school.  It is suspicious if you are asked to pay tuition for a graduate program in physics and if someone asks you to pay tuition you should usually look at different programs.

Many of the foreign students who study in the US have some contacts in the university they are applying to.  Often students are asked to apply to the program.  It can be more difficult for some students from many non-Western European universities to get in to US universities because the committee often does not know the quality of the undergraduate program the student is coming from.  This is why a lot of the foreign students have contacts in the US, or advisers in their home country who have contacts in the US.  The letters of recommendation can have a lot of weight if the admissions committee or someone on the faculty knows the reviewer and therefore has some understanding of the quality of the student.  This is true in particular for students from countries where cheating on the graduate admissions exam is common because the test scores may be suspect.  If you have an adviser with contacts in the US, you may consider applying to universities where your adviser has collaborators or contacts to increase your odds of acceptance.

Letters of recommendation are generally more positive in the US than in the rest of the world.  Admissions committees should know this and be able to somewhat take it into account.  The more specific and detailed your letters of recommendation are, the more useful they are for the committee.  It is not uncommon in other countries for students to write their own letters of recommendation, at the request of the people who should be writing these letters, and have the recommender only sign the letter.  In the US this may be considered a violation of the code of conduct of the university and if it is discovered, it may be grounds for revoking an offer of admission or even expelling a student from the university.  I would strongly recommend that you not write your own letters of recommendation.

Universities in the US have much stricter policies on cheating and academic dishonesty than some foreign institutions.  While you are usually allowed to work on homework with other students, you are not allowed to copy and you are usually implicitly forbidden from googling for answers, even though solutions are usually available for the common graduate text books.  If you get caught doing these things you can get expelled, which would cause you to lose your visa status and get kicked out of the country.  Institutions are generally not very forgiving of students who do things against the student code of conduct, even if it deviates from the norms in their home countries.  It is your responsibility to know what is considered cheating.

Unlike most European systems, not all universities in the US are of the same rank.  If you're even considering studying physics in the US, you probably know some of the most competitive programs in the US.  It can be very difficult to figure out which programs are higher ranked.  There is a ranking published by US News which you can find here.  These are generally approximately valid - #1 is universally recognized as better than #200 - but particularly in the broad middle of the distribution, the ranking is not necessarily accurate, particularly for your field.  It is usually your PhD adviser and his or her standing in the field that determines the career opportunities available for you after graduation.

Students are generally more protected in US universities than in foreign universities.  You are usually not required to stay with your adviser, even if he or she recruited you and you could switch advisers.  It usually delays graduation but can be worth it.  Adviser's behavior is limited by university regulations.  Faculty are not allowed to date students and can get fired if they do.  They are not allowed to sexually harass students.  While most graduate students are expected to work hard, faculty cannot require you to work 18 hours/day or forbid bathroom breaks or engage in any other abusive behavior.  If you get to the US and have a problem with your adviser, you should discuss this with someone at the university.  If there is another faculty member in the department you feel comfortable speaking to, this may be a good place to start.  There is also usually an office which supports foreign students which could provide some guidance and the main graduate school can also provide some support.  You do not have to put up with abusive behavior from your adviser and the system in the US is pretty good at protecting students from egregious abuses.  Too few foreign students take advantage of this.

The process of applying to graduate school in the US
Admissions deadlines - The admissions deadlines for most graduate programs in physics is usually in December or January for the semester beginning in the following August.  The exact deadlines vary.  The UTK physics program has no hard deadline, although most students who are admitted have completed their application by the end of January.
The application itself - Usually transcripts with a certified translation into English (if in a foreign language), three letters of recommendation, graduate admission exam scores, English exam scores (if you did not get a degree from an institution where the primary instruction language is English) and a personal statement are required.  As mentioned above the norm for letters of recommendation is a bit different in the US than in the rest of the world, but admissions committees know this and there's not too much you can do about it.
The personal statement is a chance for you to say why you want to go to this university and what you are interested in studying.  You should state what you want to study clearly at the beginning of this statement.  When your application is read, it will likely be forwarded to faculty who do what you want to do to see if these faculty are interested in taking you on as a student.  The easier it is to find this information, the more likely it is that you will be accepted.  It is appropriate to discuss why you want to study in the US here and what you want to do with your career.  If there are extenuating circumstance - for instance if there were a fire alarm when you took your graduate admissions exam - it is appropriate to mention it here.  There is usually a maximum length for these - it's usually better if you only use half of that space.  Be sure to get the name of the institution correct in this statement - a great student might not be rejected if he or she does this, but telling me why you want to go to Vanderbilt does not convince me that you'd be a good fit for UTK.
English in the application - You need to be able to communicate in English.  You are not expected to speak flawless English.  If your English is so poor that it raises questions about whether or not you can take and pass classes taught in English, then your English could be a problem.  Obviously the committee will have a better impression of you if you write English well and you should proof-read your application before submitting it, but you are not expected to speak or write English flawlessly.

Graduate admissions exam (GRE)
Most graduate programs in the US require both the general GRE and the physics subject GRE.  You can find out about the GRE and register for it here.  You likely have to take the GRE 6-8 months before the application deadline for the programs you are applying to.  At least a year before you want to be enrolled in graduate school in the US, you should look up the dates for both the general and subject GREs and for the TOEFL to be sure that you can take the exam.  For example, in order to enroll in grad school in the US in the Fall of 2014, for most programs you would have had to take the general exam on Nov. 9, 2013 and you would have had to register for it by Oct. 4, 2013.

Test of English as a Foreign Language (TOEFL)
You can find out about the TOEFL here.  I haven't taken the TOEFL so I can't tell you much about it.

Visas
Every graduate school has an office whose job it is to help students with the logistics of student visas.  You do not need to have a visa when you apply.  If you get accepted at a graduate school in the US and confirm that you will attend, you should look into how to get a visa soon after you've made your decision to attend school in the US.  The school cannot apply for your visa for you.  While visas usually are not a problem, particularly if it is difficult to get a visa to the US from your country, you should try to apply as early as reasonable.  Most students come in on an F1 visa.  If you are on an F1 visa, you can stay in the country for up to 1 year after you get your degree if you get a job related to your degree.  This is called optional practical training (OPT).  During that time you can apply for another kind of visa.
