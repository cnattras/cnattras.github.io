      HBT can be important, as you say, when delta-eta AND delta-phi AND delta-pT are all small.  True at all pT, and it is of course a N-particle effect so does not get “diluted” by high multiplicity.  (Not that this was an issue you raised.)

      (pTassoc-pTtrig)>300 MeV/c.

      Note that this is a pair-wise cut, so it is not enough that the *thresholds* (minimum pT) for the associated and trigger differ by more than 300 MeV, but each *pair* entering into your correlation function needs a 300+ MeV/c difference in pT.  In practical terms, it might be a good idea to just set an upper threshold on the associated particle: i.e. pTtrig>3.0 GeV/c and 1.5 < pTassoc < 2.5 GeV/c.  Certainly you won’t lose much statistics that way.

      Fig. 9 is v3 measured with the two particle cumulant method.  This is inherently contaminated by the very signal we are trying to measure.  This can be interesting for other studies, however, it is not the appropriate background for high pT dihadron correlation studies used to measure jets.  See for example the discussion in this review paper by Voloshin et al:
      "Since nonflow effects are mainly due to few particle correlations, estimates of flow coefficients based on multi-particle correlations have the distinct advantage
      of reducing nonflow correlations."
      "Nonflow dn is defined by Eq. (2). These are correlations not associated with the reaction plane. Included in nonflow effects are jets, resonance decay, short-range
      correlations such as the Hanbury-Brown Twis (HBT) effect, and momentum conservation [36, 37].  There exists several methods to evaluate and suppress nonflow contributions, such as using rapidity gaps between correlated particles, using different charge combinations for correlated particles (to assess the contribution of resonances), etc."
      See in particular section 2.4, which discusses non-flow contributions in various methods.  Fig. 9 in the paper you reference uses a two particle cumulant method with no eta gap between the particles.  This measurement will inherently include the jet signal we are trying to extract.  Of course the two particle cumulant between two tracks in the TPC shows a strong eta dependence - correlations such as jets have a strong eta dependence.  Fig. 9 is not a measurement of hydrodynamical flow.  What you see in Fig. 9 is quite simply that as an eta gap is introduced between two tracks, the non-flow correlations between particles decreases dramatically.  This is exactly the same principle that allows the background for high pT jet-like correlations to be subtracted by looking at the correlation at large d-eta.

    • 293-297 - "This" refers to the observation made in the previous sentence.
      In p+p we do not expect medium modifications.  (In somewhat of a simplification - bear with me) there are then two main types of particle production:
      1.  "soft" processes - like the underlying event, low Q^2 processes that can't be described well by pQCD
      2.  "hard" processes - high Q^2 processes and their products, like jets
      An inclusive spectrum is then a sum of particles produced by soft and hard processes.  The former is roughly exponential in pT (an observation, not a prediction) and the latter is described by a power law (from QCD - specific to the process of course but jets and heavy quarks, for instance, both have a roughly power law spectrum.)
      [Note the distinction between "hard" and "soft" is somewhat arbitrary.]
      At "low" pT we expect soft processes to dominate.  At "high" pT we expect hard processes  to dominate (mainly jets because their production cross section is larger than, say, heavy quark production.  Plus heavy quark production really is kind of a subset of jet production.)  Again, "high" and "low are kind of arbitrary, but in p+p we think (from models) that at pT~2 GeV/c more particles are from "hard" processes than from "soft" processes.

      The picture in A+A is less clear because we can have other processes and interactions with the medium.  So here we're testing whether or not there is any evidence for processes other than jet fragmentation and hadronization leading to the jet-like correlation.  An example of this is recombination of quarks in the medium to form final state hadrons hadrons.  That is, we could have an alternate particle production mechanism in the jet-like correlation in heavy ion collisions.  In heavy ion collisions, we see evidence that recombination dominates in this pT region in the bulk - if you look at ALL particles with pT ~ 2 GeV/c, it looks like most of them are formed by recombination.  However, if we look just at particles in the jet-like correlation - particles we think are formed from the fragmentation and hadronization of hard quarks or gluons - these particles look just like those in p+p, so we think this is evidence that the jet-like correlation DOES NOT form through recombination.
